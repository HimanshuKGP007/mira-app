Mira · module 1 — the scoring engine

Inside the phoneme scoring engine: one word, sound by sound

This walks the word "sun" through the exact pipeline Mira runs on every recording — from raw audio to a per-sound verdict — and shows where the engineering decisions live that a generic speech-recognition tool doesn't have to make.

The pipeline, at a glance

Each numbered stage below is expanded step by step underneath.

01 dictionary lookup 02 audio in 03 acoustic model 04 forced alignment 05 goodness-of-pronunciation 06 marking decision 07 per-sound result
"sun" → s ʌ n
STEP 01

The target is known in advance

Mira already knows the word it asked for, so it looks up "sun" in a pronunciation dictionary and gets the exact three sounds — s, ʌ, n — that a correct answer should contain.

This is the fork in the road versus ordinary speech recognition. A voice assistant doesn't know what you meant to say, so it's built to guess past your mispronunciation and still get the word right — the error gets smoothed away before it's ever visible. Mira needs the opposite: the word is fixed, so every deviation from these three sounds is signal, not noise to correct for.

raw recording · resampled to 16kHz mono
STEP 02

The recording is checked before it's trusted

Before any scoring happens, Mira measures the signal-to-noise ratio of the clip. Recordings that are too quiet, too short, or too noisy to measure reliably are rejected here and the child is asked to try again — never scored and silently marked wrong.

This matters because a bad recording that gets scored anyway looks exactly like a bad pronunciation. Catching it before the expensive modelling step also keeps the app fast.

model's frame-by-frame read of the audio, lined up against s · ʌ · n
STEP 03 – 04

The audio is measured in 20ms slices, and lined up against the target

A neural acoustic model listens to the whole clip and produces, for every 20-millisecond slice, a probability for every possible sound in its vocabulary — not just the three we asked about.

Those slices are then lined up against the target sequence s → ʌ → n using forced alignment, so Mira knows which stretch of audio corresponds to which intended sound. The highlighted bars above show where the model's confidence spikes for each target; the dip in the middle is the weak ʌ.

STEP 05

Goodness-of-pronunciation: how much belief landed on the right sound

For each of the three windows, Mira reads off how much of the model's probability mass actually landed on the target sound, against every other sound it could have been. That's the "GOP" (goodness-of-pronunciation) score — 0.96 for s, 0.41 for ʌ, 0.88 for n in this take.

A plain average across the word would report "75% — pretty good." GOP scored per sound is what makes it possible to say instead: "the /ʌ/ sound is the one that needs work, not the whole word."

STEP 06

A number becomes a verdict, through a fixed set of checks

A raw probability isn't shown to anyone directly — it's run through an ordered set of checks that decide what actually happened: was the sound simply not attempted (omitted), was a different sound said instead (substituted), or was it produced correctly?

For ʌ here, the model's own unprompted read of that stretch of audio recognised something else there — consistent with the low target probability — so the verdict is substituted, not a vague low score.

STEP 07

What comes out the other end

One record per sound: whether it was correct, what it was heard as instead if not, and a confidence figure for the measurement itself — the whole thing runs in roughly a tenth of a second per word.

Nowhere in this output is a diagnosis, a severity rating, or a recommendation. That boundary is enforced in code, not just in how the results are displayed.

Why this is hard to copy

Phoneme-level pronunciation scoring itself is an established technique — the underlying "goodness-of-pronunciation" method has existed in speech research for two decades. What's built on top of it here is where the actual edge sits.

Path A — Broad (live today)

Did they say the right sound?

The GOP method walked through above. Catches swapped and dropped sounds reliably.

  • Ranks a bad sound below a good one correctly 84% of the time in held-out testing
  • Blind to one failure mode: a sound produced in the right "slot" but distorted — e.g. a lisped /s/ still reads as /s/
live in the product
Path B — Correlate (built, not yet turned on)

Was it physically produced correctly?

Direct acoustic measurement — tongue and airflow physics, not just "which sound did the model recognise."

  • Catches exactly what Path A can't: right sound, wrong physical production
  • The code exists and runs end-to-end; the reference ranges it compares against haven't been clinically validated yet
built, not yet validated

Running both paths and combining them — catching swaps, drops, and distortions in one measurement — is the part that isn't commodity. Most shipped pronunciation tools stop at Path A.

What's actually novel here

✓ The real edge

  • The two-path design (broad + correlate) running as one system, built to catch distortions that GOP-only tools structurally cannot see
  • Measured, published-grade accent-fairness evidence for Indian English specifically — the 11.2% vs 16.0% result above, which took a dedicated validation stage to establish
  • No Montreal Forced Aligner subprocess — the alignment step runs natively, which is what keeps the whole pipeline deployable at ~120ms per word with no external service
  • A hard-coded boundary between measurement and clinical judgment, enforced structurally rather than by convention

Established prior art

  • Goodness-of-pronunciation scoring itself — a known technique in speech research since the early 2000s, used by several existing pronunciation apps
  • Using a neural acoustic model plus forced alignment to score speech — standard practice in the field
📌

Where this stands today: the broad path (Path A) is live and validated against held-out speakers with the numbers above. The correlate path (Path B) — the part that would let Mira also catch distorted-but-recognisable sounds — is fully built but its reference ranges are not yet clinically validated. All of this has been tested on adult and adolescent second-language English learners saying prompted words — not yet on children's speech or disordered speech, which is the next phase of validation.
